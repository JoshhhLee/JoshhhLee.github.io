Segmentation Lab

Lab 01 asked what a convolution does to an image. This one asks a harder question: how do you label every single pixel, how do you know whether you got it right, and how do you get the resolution back after a network has thrown it away?

What the network is actually punished for

IoU tells you how good a prediction is, but you cannot train on it directly — it is not differentiable. So networks are trained on a stand-in. The three below make very different bargains, and the one you pick decides what the network learns to ignore.

Per-pixel behaviour

Cross-entropy and focal are per-pixel losses, so a per-pixel curve is an honest picture of them. Dice is deliberately absent — it has no per-pixel value to plot.

This is not academic. In camouflaged object detection the animal may be two percent of the pixels, so cross-entropy's easy background wins swamp the gradient from the handful of pixels that matter. Dice normalises that away; focal loss starves the easy pixels of gradient on purpose. Most working systems use cross-entropy and Dice added together.

Getting the resolution back

A classifier's job is to destroy spatial information — pool it away until only "cat" remains. Segmentation needs it back at full resolution. There are three ways to enlarge a feature map, and only one of them has anything to learn.

Nearest and bilinear are fixed rules — they invent no detail, they only choose between the values they were given. The transposed convolution has actual weights, and in a real network those weights are learned. They are set by hand here so you can compare two specific choices, and the next section walks through how they land on the output.

Transposed convolution, one cell at a time

A normal convolution gathers: many input cells collapse into one output. A transposed convolution scatters: each input cell paints a whole copy of the kernel into the output, and wherever those copies land on top of each other the values add up. Step through it and watch the overlaps build — the uneven ones are the checkerboard artifact being born.

Five
架 · 橋

What the skip connections were carrying

U-Net's shape gets drawn in every lecture, but the arrows across the middle are usually waved at. Here is what they do. The encoder halves the image three times; the decoder puts it back. Switch a skip off and the detail that level was carrying is gone for good — there is nowhere else in the network it could have come from.

Read this as an analogy, not a simulation. There are no learned weights here — the encoder is average pooling and the decoder is bilinear upsampling, so with every skip connected the reconstruction comes back exactly. A trained U-Net never does that: its convolutions transform features rather than preserving them. What transfers is the structural point — a skip is the only route by which fine detail can reach the decoder — and that point is real. Section six of lab 01, and the training section below, are where actual weights get learned.

Now train it to find the animal

Everything above was analysis. This is a real network — two convolutional layers, a ReLU between them, a sigmoid at the end, weights in total — learning by gradient descent, in your browser, right now. Its job is the one from my own research: there is a shape hidden in that texture, and almost nothing separates it from the background except statistics.

One result worth keeping. Focal loss here uses α = 0.5. The value quoted almost everywhere is α = 0.25, from the RetinaNet paper — and measured on this task it makes focal markedly worse, because it down-weights exactly the rare class we are trying to find. Detection and segmentation are not the same problem, and a hyperparameter that is standard in one is not automatically right in the other. Inherit numbers from papers with care, and measure them on your own data.
This is a two-layer network with a 5×5 effective receptive field and no pooling, so do not read too much into the absolute IoU — a real segmenter sees far more context. What does transfer is the comparison: same network, same data, same initialisation, only the objective changed.

Boxes, and throwing most of them away

Detection answers a coarser question than segmentation — where is it, roughly — but the same IoU appears again, this time with a closed form for rectangles. A detector proposes hundreds of overlapping boxes, and non-maximum suppression is the almost embarrassingly simple rule that cleans them up.

Box IoU — drag either box
Non-maximum suppression IoU threshold
Lower the threshold and NMS gets aggressive — two genuinely separate animals standing close together get merged into one detection. Raise it and duplicates survive. There is no correct value, only a value that suits your data, which is why it is a tuned hyperparameter rather than a constant.
