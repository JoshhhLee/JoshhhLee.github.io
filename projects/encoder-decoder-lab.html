Encoder–Decoder Lab

Lab 02 built a U-Net and trained it. This one is about everything people change afterwards: swapping in a pretrained backbone, redesigning the decoder, widening the receptive field without destroying resolution, and bolting attention onto the parts that need it.

An encoder is a feature pyramid

A backbone is not a black box that emits "features". It is a staircase: at every step the map gets smaller, the channel count grows, and each position sees more of the original image. Segmentation lives on the tension between those last two — deep features know what, shallow features know where, and no single stage knows both.

What a pretrained encoder actually buys

The argument for pretrained backbones is usually made with a citation. Here it is made with a measurement — and the measurement does not say what you have been told it says. Same network, same camouflage task as lab 02. The only difference is what the first layer's weights are on iteration zero.

"Scratch" starts from random noise. "Pretrained" starts from six classical filters — edges, a Laplacian, a blur, a centre-surround — which is close to what the first layer of a trained vision network converges to anyway. Run both. Why the demo refuses to show the effect you were promised. Measured over five seeds before this page shipped: at full learning rate scratch reached IoU 0.65 and pretrained 0.57; frozen, 0.52 and 0.51; at a tenth encoder rate, 0.66 and 0.69. Pretraining never reliably wins. That is not a broken demo — it is a network with 77 parameters training on a single image for unlimited epochs. None of the conditions that make transfer learning valuable are present. Transfer pays when data is scarce relative to model capacity, and here data is effectively infinite relative to a very small model.
So what does a real pretrained backbone give you? Not better first-layer filters — those are cheap, as you have just watched a network rediscover them in a few hundred iterations. What you cannot afford to train yourself is everything section one was about: depth. A ResNet-50 encoder hands you five stages, a receptive field covering most of the image, and 23 million parameters shaped by a million labelled photographs. With a few hundred medical scans or camouflage images you could never fit that from scratch; it would memorise the training set before it learned anything general. You are borrowing the hierarchy, not the edge detectors.
The controls are still the real ones. Set the encoder rate to zero and you have frozen the backbone — the standard recipe when data is very scarce. Note that freezing costs you something even here: about 0.13 IoU against a fully trainable encoder, because the head alone cannot compensate for features that were never adapted to the task. The range in between is "differential learning rates", a small rate for the encoder and a full one for the decoder, which is what most fine-tuning scripts are quietly doing.

ASPP — look at every scale at once

If one dilation rate gives you one scale, the obvious move is to run several in parallel and let the network combine them. That is Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling, the centrepiece of DeepLab. Switch branches off and watch the fused map lose the scale each one was providing.

Why toggling image pooling does not change the fused picture. That branch averages the entire map to a single number and broadcasts it back, so it is spatially constant — it carries global context, not local structure. Real ASPP concatenates its branches into separate channels rather than adding them, so that constant survives as its own channel for later layers to use. The preview here sums the dilated branches to keep them comparable in one image, and leaves the pooling branch out, because adding a constant to every pixel would change nothing you could see.

Squeeze-and-excitation, then CBAM

A convolutional layer treats all its channels as equally important. They are not. SE asks a simple question — averaged over the whole image, how strongly did each channel fire? — and uses the answer to turn channels up or down. CBAM then adds the same idea across space: which locations matter.

Attention on the skip connection

Lab 02 established that skip connections are the only route by which fine detail reaches the decoder. That is also their problem: they forward everything at that resolution, including a great deal of background the decoder has no use for. Attention U-Net puts a gate on the skip — the decoder's coarse, semantically confident guess decides which parts of the fine-grained skip are allowed through.

This is the bridge to lab 05. A camouflaged animal is precisely the case where the skip connection is carrying an overwhelming amount of background that looks statistically identical to the target. Gating the skip is one of the main things SINet and its descendants are doing, under various names. Push the coarse-guess quality down and watch the gate confidently suppress the wrong region — a gate is only ever as good as the semantics driving it.
